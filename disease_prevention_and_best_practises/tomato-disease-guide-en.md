# Tomato Leaf Disease Guide
### Septoria Leaf Spot · Bacterial Leaf Spot · Early Blight · Late Blight

*A simple field guide for identifying, preventing, and managing the four most common tomato leaf diseases.*

> **Important note before you use this guide:** This guide covers general, widely-recommended practices. Exact fungicide/bactericide products and dosages vary by state and change over time as regulations update — always confirm the specific product and dose with your nearest **Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK)** or state agricultural extension officer, and follow the product label exactly. This guide does not replace that advice; it prepares you to have a more useful conversation with your local expert.

---

## 1. Septoria Leaf Spot

**What it looks like:** Small, circular brown-to-tan spots on the leaves, usually starting on the lower/older leaves near the ground. Look closely at the center of a spot — Septoria often shows tiny black dots (like pepper flecks) in the middle, which is the clearest way to tell it apart from early blight. It does not infect the fruit or stem, only the leaves.

**Conditions that favor it:** Moderate temperatures (20–25°C) combined with long, continuous periods of leaf wetness — rain, dew, or overhead watering that keeps the leaves wet for many hours in a row. A single wet day is a weak signal; several wet days in a row is when this disease actually takes hold.

**What to do:**
- **Irrigation:** Switch to drip irrigation or water at the base of the plant. If you must use overhead watering, do it early in the morning so leaves dry out during the day — never water in the evening.
- **Spacing & mulch:** Space plants well apart and mulch the soil around the base. This stops rain from splashing soil (where the fungus survives between seasons) up onto the leaves.
- **Sanitation:** Remove and destroy infected lower leaves as soon as you see them. Clear away all old plant debris at the end of the season — this fungus survives in dead leaves left in the field.
- **Treatment:** Copper-based fungicides are commonly effective against this disease when applied early, before the infection spreads. Ask your local KVK for the currently approved product and dose.

## 2. Early Blight

**What it looks like:** Circular brown spots, usually larger than Septoria's, with a distinctive **target pattern** — rings inside rings, like a dartboard — often surrounded by a yellow ring. Starts on the oldest, lowest leaves first and moves upward. In advanced cases it can also affect stems and fruit, causing dark sunken patches.

**Conditions that favor it:** Warm temperatures (24–29°C) combined with high humidity, especially when wet and dry periods alternate. Plants that are already stressed (nutrient-deficient, drought-stressed, or overloaded with fruit) are more vulnerable.

**What to do:**
- **Irrigation:** Same as Septoria — drip irrigation over overhead watering, and never water late in the day.
- **Soil & nutrition:** Avoid excess nitrogen fertilizer — it produces lush, soft leaf growth that is more susceptible to infection. Use a balanced fertilizer, and well-decomposed farmyard manure or compost rather than fresh manure.
- **Crop rotation:** Don't plant tomatoes (or related crops like potato, brinjal/eggplant, chili) in the same field for at least 2 years. This fungus survives in soil and old plant debris between seasons.
- **Pruning & staking:** Stake or trellis plants and prune the lowest leaves so air moves freely through the plant and leaves dry faster.
- **Treatment:** Fungicides containing chlorothalonil or mancozeb are commonly used preventatively, applied before symptoms appear or at the very first sign of disease, and reapplied on a regular interval (check locally-approved intervals). Copper-based products also help. Once a leaf is heavily infected, spraying won't cure that leaf — the goal is protecting healthy growth.

## 3. Bacterial Leaf Spot

**What it looks like:** Small spots (much smaller than early blight — about the size of a pinhead), often appearing water-soaked or greasy when held up to light, especially on the underside of the leaf. As spots age, the center can dry out and fall away, leaving a "shot-hole" look. Unlike Septoria, this disease **does** affect the fruit, causing small raised dark scabs.

**Conditions that favor it:** Warm temperatures (24–30°C) combined with actual rain or wind-driven splash events — this disease needs a physical splashing/wounding event to spread, not just humid air sitting still.

**What to do:**
- **This is the hardest of the four to treat once established — prevention matters more here than for any other disease on this list**, because there is currently no fully resistant commercial tomato variety and no true "cure" once a plant is infected.
- **Irrigation:** Avoid overhead irrigation entirely if at all possible — this is the single most important step for this disease specifically, since splashing water is exactly how it spreads.
- **Seed & seedling source:** Use certified, disease-free seed or seedlings from a reputable nursery. This disease can travel in on seed.
- **Hygiene:** Don't work in the field or handle plants when foliage is wet (early morning dew, right after rain) — bacteria spread easily on wet hands, tools, and clothing. Disinfect tools and stakes between uses.
- **Treatment:** Copper-based bactericide sprays are the main chemical option and work best as prevention, applied before an outbreak or at the earliest sign, not as a cure for existing spots. True antibiotics are not commonly used or recommended for field crops in most regions — check current local guidance.

## 4. Late Blight

**What it looks like:** The most destructive of the four, and the one that spreads fastest. Pale green-to-brown, water-soaked patches, often with a **light green-to-yellow halo**, that can appear on leaves at any position on the plant — not just the lower/older leaves like the other three diseases. In humid conditions you may see white, fuzzy fungal growth on the underside of affected leaves. Can destroy an entire field within days under the right conditions.

**Conditions that favor it:** Cool temperatures (15–22°C) combined with high humidity (above 85–90%) and extended wetness. This is the opposite temperature pattern from early blight and bacterial spot — late blight likes it cooler, not warmer.

**What to do:**
- **Treat any suspected late blight as an emergency** — because of how fast it spreads, don't wait to "see if it gets worse." Report it to your local agricultural extension office immediately; some regions track outbreaks regionally because of how contagious this disease is between neighboring farms.
- **Irrigation:** Drip irrigation, same as above. Avoid overhead watering, especially in cool weather.
- **Remove infected material immediately:** Pull and destroy (don't compost) any severely infected plants to stop the disease from spreading to neighboring plants.
- **Resistant varieties:** Some tomato varieties with better late blight tolerance exist — ask your local KVK which varieties are recommended and available for your region and season.
- **Treatment:** This disease moves faster than the other three, so preventive fungicide programs (applied before symptoms appear, during known high-risk weather) are far more effective than trying to treat it after an outbreak starts. Ask your local extension officer about currently recommended products for your area, as resistance management and locally approved chemistries change often for this particular disease.

## General Practices That Help Against All Four Diseases

**Irrigation**
- Drip irrigation (or a soaker hose, or watering at the base by hand) is better than overhead sprinkler watering for every disease on this list — wet leaves are the common thread that spreads all four.
- If overhead watering is your only option, always water early in the morning so leaves have the whole day to dry.
- Avoid watering in the evening — leaves that stay wet all night create the exact conditions these diseases need.

**Soil health & manure**
- Use well-decomposed farmyard manure (FYM) or compost, not fresh/raw manure — fresh manure can carry pathogens and tends to release nitrogen too quickly, producing soft, disease-prone growth.
- Avoid over-fertilizing with nitrogen. A balanced approach (adequate potassium in particular supports the plant's own disease resistance) is healthier than pushing for maximum leafy growth.
- Vermicompost and neem cake are commonly used soil-health inputs in Indian farming that can support overall plant vigor, alongside — not instead of — the other practices here.

**Field hygiene**
- Rotate tomatoes (and related crops — potato, brinjal, chili) out of a field for at least 2 years if any of these diseases have appeared before.
- Remove and destroy infected plant material — don't leave it in the field, and don't add heavily diseased material to your regular compost pile.
- Space plants well and prune lower leaves to keep air moving through the crop canopy.

**When in doubt**
- These four diseases can look similar in early stages, especially to the naked eye. If you're not sure which one you're looking at, use the CropGuard scan feature for a second opinion, and don't hesitate to bring a sample to your local KVK for confirmation before spraying anything.
